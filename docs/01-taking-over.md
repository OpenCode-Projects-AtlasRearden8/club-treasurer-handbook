# Chapter 1 — Taking Over Without Dread

*Or: what to do in your first two weeks when the shoebox arrives.*

Somewhere between "Sure, I can be treasurer" and the first meeting where
someone hands you a coffee can of cash, there's a gap. This chapter closes
that gap. The trick is to treat handover like moving into an apartment: you
inspect, you inventory, you change the mindset from "previous person's mess" to
"my clean slate."

## Step 1: Get the keys (the money

Ask for these five things before anything else:

1. **Bank access.** Every account the club has, online login and debit
   cards/checkbooks included. If a card or login was in the previous
   treasurer's name, start the process of moving it to your name (or the
   club's) now — this is the single slowest part of the whole job.
2. **The ledger.** Whatever the club uses — a notebook, a spreadsheet, an app
   export. Take the latest copy.
3. **Unreconciled paper.** Any receipts, deposit slips, outstanding checks,
   or IOU notes lying around.
4. **The dues list.** Who's paid, who hasn't, who's grandfathered into some
   rate nobody remembers.
5. **Signatory paperwork.** If the bank requires two signatures on checks,
   know who the second signatory is and whether the bank form is current.

> Tap the bank's "add/remove signer" process early. It usually requires the
> outgoing treasurer to show up in person or sign something. Weeks of
> politeness are worth it here.

## Step 2: Count the money (with a witness)

Do your starting cash count **with someone else watching** — ideally the club
president, or whoever's handing off. You're both there to say "yes, that's what
was there." Count bills, count the petty cash drawer if there is one, and write
the total down with both your initials. This one awkward half-hour protects
both you and your predecessor. Nobody ever argues with "we counted together
and signed it."

If cash amounts don't match the books, don't panic and don't accuse. Write
down the difference, note it as an adjustment, and keep going. Discrepancies of
small amounts happen. Big discrepancies go to the club president quietly.

## Step 3: Read the last three months of activity

Before you change *anything*, spend thirty minutes reading the ledger for the
last couple of months. You're looking for:

- **Recurring money:** dues coming in, monthly subscriptions, rent for the
  meeting space, insurance (if any).
- **Recurring spending:** what the club spends on every meeting, in a normal
  month.
- **Anything weird:** transfers to people you don't recognize, payments to
  someone *twice*, large cash withdrawals. Ask the previous treasurer or
  president about those — most are innocent ("oh, that's the trophy fund"),
  but you should know before money moves again.

## Step 4: Decide what "the books" will be (yours now)

You get to pick the system. The bar for a good system is:

- One page of truth (a ledger) where **every** dollar in and out gets a line.
- Two bank accounts (see [Chapter 2](02-two-accounts.html)).
- Paper attachments (receipts) that live in one place and get filed monthly.

May we humbly suggest [the starter ledger template](../templates/ledger.csv)?
It's a plain CSV — it opens in any spreadsheet program. Copy it, rename it
`ledger.csv` (or keep it in a spreadsheet), and start. If you prefer pen and
paper, copy the columns onto notebook pages in the same order. The *format*
matters less than the *consistency*.

## Step 5: Make a one-page letter to your future self

Write (or email) yourself a short note now, while everything is fresh:

- Account names, numbers *masked* (last 4 digits only!), and what each is for.
- Who has access to what.
- Where the paper files live.
- Anything weird you found and what you did about it.

This note becomes the seed of your handoff binder (Chapter 7) a year from now.
It's also your "what on earth is account 'Old Reserve 2'?" answer when you
forget in March.

## Things people worry about that they shouldn't

- **"I'm not good at math."** This job is counting, adding, and comparing. If
  you can split a pizza bill for five people without an argument, you can do
  this.
- **"What if the numbers don't match?"** They often won't, at first. Reconciling
  is just noticing and fixing — it's a core part of Chapter 3's monthly rhythm,
  and it gets easier every month.
- **"I don't know what I don't know."** Correct. That's why Chapter 2 insists
  on separation of accounts and Chapter 8 builds fraud-proofing into the
  routine. You don't need to know everything on day 1; you need the structure
  that keeps you from caring about the things you don't know.

## Things people worry about that they *should*

- **Access in someone else's name.** Fix it this month. When accounts,
  cards, and logins still belong to "Treasurer, 2019-2023," your club is
  trusting a ghost.
- **No paper trail at all.** You don't need to reconstruct history perfectly —
  but from day 1, *your* paper trail must be complete.
- **Missing monthly report habit.** If the club has gone years without one,
  start this month. Doesn't matter that last month wasn't reported; matters
  that this month is.

## Your first-two-weeks checklist

- [ ] Request bank access and start name-transfer paperwork
- [ ] Get the ledger, dues list, and any loose paper from the previous treasurer
- [ ] Count cash with a witness; sign and date the count
- [ ] Read the last few months of activity; ask about anything odd
- [ ] Choose your ledger format and copy the starter template
- [ ] Write yourself the one-page "keys" note (masked numbers)
- [ ] Announce — one paragraph at a meeting — that reports will go out monthly, and when

That last one matters more than it sounds. Once people know to expect a
one-pager on, say, the 15th, the habit is external pressure instead of
internal willpower. You've just outsourced your discipline to the club.
Good treasurers outsource *everything* they can.
