Chapter 8 — Fraud-Proofing Basics

Make stealing boringly hard, so being trusted stays easy.

One uncomfortable fact up front, stated kindly: almost all club money loss isn't a masked stranger — it's a trusted insider under pressure. Good people, real-life trouble, a loophole nobody closed. Fraud-proofing isn't about suspecting your friends; it's about building a system where nobody has to be suspected.

The good news: at club scale, four habits do almost all of the work, and three of them are things this handbook already had you doing.

The four habits

1. Two-eyeballs on every payout

No payout — reimbursement, vendor check, transfer — is arranged by one person alone. The spender isn't the payer; the payer isn't the spender. You + an approver covers it. This single rule removes the "one person moves money quietly" shape that almost all insider loss needs to survive.

(Yes, at small scale that means boring approvals. Yes, that's the price, and it's cheaper than the alternative.)

2. Statements seen by more than the treasurer

Every month, one other officer (president is plenty) glances at the bank statement — or the report's numbers — and says something out loud like "looks right." Thirty seconds. This is the cheapest anti-fraud tool in existence, because anything needing the treasurer's silence to grow gets interrupted monthly.

3. The paper trail is complete (Chapter 3 is the wall)

A receipt and a ledger line for every dollar. Missing paper is how loss-rivers find their banks — a $63 lunch with no receipt each month is $756/year, invisible until somebody asks in year three. Your ledger and receipt folder are the wall.

4. Access shrinks when people's roles end

The moment someone's treasurer/officer term ends: remove their bank access, block their card, rotate the passwords they had. Chapter 7 makes it a handoff step; treat it like returning keys to a building. Also covers the sad-but-common case of the former treasurer who simply disappears.

Fast early-warning signs (worth knowing without paranoia)

  • A member or officer resists the paper trail specifically. ("Just pay me back cash next time" is very common — normalizing it is the risk.)
  • Unexplained balance shortfalls in months with no expenses to explain them.
  • One person insisting on handling everything money, all the time — including being the only one who "understands" the books.
  • Vendors or invoices you haven't heard of showing up in the ledger.
  • Round-number, recurring, small expenses that no remembered event explains.

None of these mean guilt; each means look once, kindly. Most check out.

If it happens to your club (calm playbook)

  1. Write down what you actually know vs. suspect. Facts with dates.
  2. Loop in one more officer (the president) — this stops being a solo-correction job the moment it's real.
  3. Protect the money first: rotate bank access/passwords, block cards, freeze further payouts until count-and-reconcile is done.
  4. Count and reconcile. The gap either shows up as a clean number or proves unfounded. Chapter 3's monthly audit makes this an afternoon, not a forensic expedition.
  5. If confirmed, decide as a club what happens — repayment, police report, or informal resolution is a club policy decision, not a treasurer decree. Small, honest conversations beat scorched earth for small clubs.
  6. Patch the loophole that let it happen, warmly, on the record. "Two people now verify payouts" is a stronger club, not an insulted one.

Digital hygiene (brief, and enough at club scale)

  • Bank logins are personal, per person, never shared. If the bank allows multiple users, each person logs in as themselves.
  • Two-factor authentication (2FA) everywhere it's offered — the bank, email, the club's payment apps. A USB-stick or authenticator app beats SMS where possible, but SMS beats nothing.
  • Beware payment-redirect scams. A "vendor" emails: "our bank details changed — pay this new account." Verify by a phone number you find independently, never by replying. This scam targets treasurers constantly because it works.
  • Email is not a bank. Nobody moves money on the strength of one email. A verbally confirmed or in-person agreed detail at minimum.
  • The club doesn't need your debit card number, and the treasurer needs only what the club needs. Least access wins.

The annual audit-lite (your successor will thank you)

Once a year, spend one hour:

  • Verify who has bank access; remove anyone who doesn't need it
  • Rotate passwords nobody's rotated; re-check 2FA is on everywhere
  • Spot-check 3 random payments: receipt, approval, ledger line all present
  • Confirm the signer lists at the bank match the current officers
  • Re-read Chapter 3 checklist and Chapter 7 checklist; fix anything rattle-y

That hour is the price of a club where the answer to "who watches the treasurer?" is calmly: "the club does — and it's no big deal."

The friendly wind-down

Every rule in this chapter has a theme: no single person alone, no dollar without paper, no access outliving the role. That's it. That's the whole secret. Run those three, stay kind, stay boring — and the worst thing that will ever happen to your club's money is a slow year.
