Chapter 5 — Paying Vendors Fairly

Fair rates, real quotes, one paper trail.

Clubs buy things: venue rentals, speakers, shirts, food, repairs, web hosting. "Fair-pay-for-vendors" means the vendor gets paid what was agreed, on time, and the club can show what the deal was — even two treasurers from now. It is equal treatment, documented, boring in the best way.

The five questions every vendor payment should be able to answer

  1. What was bought? A description a stranger understands.
  2. From whom? Vendor name and contact, on file.
  3. For how much, agreed when? A quote, an invoice, a written message — anything dated and written.
  4. Proof of delivery/pricing? The invoice or formal quote (see exceptions).
  5. Who approved it? A meeting note, an email, a signature — the club, not just you.

If all five are on paper, you have a vendor record, and you've done the whole job. The vendor payment log template captures all five as columns. Done.

Quotes before anything big, from more than one vendor

Set a dollar threshold with your club — a common, easy one at club scale is $100: anything over it gets two quotes. Nothing personal; it's just the written habit that keeps rates fair and removes any appearance of playing favorites (including with a vendor who is also a club member's cousin).

  • Write to two vendors with the same one-paragraph description of what's needed. Same paragraph — that's what makes the comparison honest.
  • Save both quotes to the files, name them quote-a.pdf/quote-b.pdf, and record the chosen one in the log with a one-line reason ("B was $40 cheaper, same specs").
  • For recurring purchases (venue rental, monthly snacks), one written comparison per year is plenty: re-quote annually, then ride the standing rate the other eleven months.

Invoices, deposits, and paying on time

  • Invoice fundamentals: date, vendor, description, amount, and how to pay. If it lacks any of these, ask the vendor to reissue — friendly note, takes one email.
  • Match before paying: the amount you pay must match the invoice; the invoice must match the approved purchase (or the vendor's quote that got chosen). A mismatch is a question moment — pause and ask the vendor. Wrong-account typos happen even to nice people.
  • Deposits: never more than half up front, in writing what the deposit covers ("$150 deposit on the $380 room rental, refundable until April 1"). Record it in the ledger as an advance against that vendor, not as an expense — the full expense is booked when the thing actually happens.
  • Pay on the invoice's clock. Vendors who get paid on time give small clubs better prices next year. It is genuinely that simple.

Paying the vendor (and paper-trailing it)

Pay exactly how the invoice says — a check, a transfer, the club card — then:

  1. Ledger line, same day, description naming the vendor, linked by name.
  2. Receipt/invoice saved to the vendor file, named with the date (same convention as receipts in Chapter 3).
  3. The payment appears (respecting rounding and grouping rules) in that month's report.

When the vendor is a club member

This deserves its own paragraph because it's the #1 way club money feels unfair even when the amounts are tiny. Rules of thumb:

  • Same quotes rule applies. If your member is willing, get a competing quote too. It's a two-email chore that defuses the whole debate.
  • Pay market rate, not relationship rate, and name the rate the same way you'd name any vendor's.
  • Someone other than the member-friend approves it — the two-eyeballs rule from Chapter 3 is hardly negotiable here.
  • The member's relationship is fine to say out loud in the minute/meeting ("Sam's shop quoted us $200 for the banner"). Hidden relationships look worse than disclosed ones, always.

W-9s, 1099s, and other "letters-and-numbers" territory

Some payments to individuals (speakers, performers, freelancers) trigger form-filing territory in some jurisdictions. This handbook will not attempt to explain it, because getting it wrong has consequences and getting it right is a professional's specialty. The educational, safe version:

  • Keep the vendor's name and contact for every payment above your threshold (the log does this).
  • If anyone asks "does this need a tax form?" — that is a tax question. Ask a tax professional (a CPA or enrolled agent), not a club-by-club guess. Ask before the event, not during December panic season; the professional will need lead time.

Chapter checklist

  • Vendor log in use; every payment answers the five questions
  • Written quotes for anything over the club's threshold (default $100)
  • Invoice matched, then paid on time; ledger line same day
  • Deposits capped at half, in writing, booked as advances
  • Member-vendors: disclosed, market rate, separately approved
  • Tax-form questions routed to a tax professional, not guessed
