# Chapter 3 — Receipts and Reimbursements

*Two habits. That's the whole chapter.*

The treasurer's daily job is almost entirely two habits: **every dollar gets
a line in the ledger**, and **every payout has a receipt and eyes on it**.
Master these and every other chapter is easy. Skip them and every other chapter
is impossible.

## Habit 1: The ledger line (30 seconds per transaction)

Whenever money moves — in or out — it gets one line in the ledger:

| date | description | category | account | money in | money out | receipt? | who paid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2026-03-07 | dues — March game night | dues | operating | 60.00 | | yes | (n/a) |
| 2026-03-07 | snacks for game night | events | operating | | 23.42 | yes | J. Rivera |

Rules that keep the ledger honest:

- **Same day, or as close to it as humanly possible.** Same-day entries take
  30 seconds. Week-old entries take ten minutes of guessing. Month-old entries
  take an evening of archaeology.
- **Write the description like a stranger will read it.** "Dinner" is a mystery
  novel. "Officer dinner with guest speaker (4 people)" is a future ally.
- **One line per transaction.** Don't batch "March stuff, $114" — nobody,
  including you, can reconcile a batch later.
- **The `who paid` column is your reimbursement memory.** When J. Rivera comes
  to you three weeks later holding a crumpled receipt, you'll know whether
  they've already been paid.
- **Category list stays short.** Four to eight categories is plenty: dues,
  events, supplies, venue, food/drink, equipment, gifts/awards, misc. If a
  category name could be two things, it's misc.

## Habit 2: The receipt rule

**No receipt, no payout. Not "probably," not "I'll get it eventually" — no
receipt, no payout, until the receipt appears.** This is the kindest rule in
the book because it protects *people* as much as money: a member whose
reimbursement gets questioned months later is having a bad month; a member who
can pull up the paper trail on the spot is having a fine one.

- **Paper receipts:** snapshot/photograph or scan them, then file the paper in
  the binder (or a folder by month). Digital receipts: save the email, save
  the screenshot, file it.
- **The receipt photo goes in the same folder the ledger line goes in.** Name
  files like `2026-03-07-snacks-rivera.jpg` so they sort themselves. (No last
  names fine if you prefer — `member1.jpg` works too. Whatever you'll find in
  six months.)
- **Lost receipt rescue:** if it's truly gone, a written note from the spender
  ("I paid $23.42 for snacks on 3/7, receipt lost, signed") plus approval from
  someone other than the spender keeps the trail honest. It's the adult
  version of the hall pass.
- **Personal cards and club money don't mix.** Cash or a club card directly
  when possible; reimbursement (next section) when a member fronts the money.

## Reimbursements, done right (built on the template)

Reimbursement is where trust lives or dies, so we formalize it. The template
(`templates/reimbursement-request.md`) is one screen long and asks:

1. **Who** is asking (name, contact).
2. **What** it was for, in one honest sentence.
3. **When** it happened (date).
4. **How much** — itemized if more than a couple lines.
5. **Receipt attached.**
6. **Approver** — a club officer *other than the spender* signs/nods.

The flow: member fills the one-pager, attaches the receipt, hands it to an
approver; the approver checks it against the obvious questions (Is this
something the club decided to buy? reads like a real purchase?) and then you
pay from Operating and record it. Two people other than the spender have
touched the request. That's not bureaucracy — that's two friendly eyeballs,
and it means nobody, including you, can accidentally self-deal.

## Reimbursement edge cases (they will happen)

- **Expenses the club didn't approve in advance.** If it's small and clearly
  in the club's line, approve it and note "approved after the fact" *on the
  record*. If it's large or out-of-scope, decline kindly and point at the
  budget process, not the person. "The way we say yes is X" beats "No."
- **Members paying each other back directly.** Discourage gently. Direct
  payments bypass the paper trail; even a small unofficial loan between
  members is their business, but the *club* only settles via the form.
- **Partial receipts.** "The gas station receipt doesn't show what I bought."
  Itemization or a note from spender + independent approval. Same bar as a
  lost receipt.
- **Tips, taxes, and rounding.** Pay the receipt total. Don't round up for
  goodwill. Rounding-up funds go in as donations with a line of their own.

## Petty cash (you've been warned)

If a previous treasurer left a petty cash box, you may keep it or abolish it.
The abolitionist argument is strong: cards and reimbursements cover nearly
everything now. If you keep it:

- It has one job (small meeting expenses) and a float of a fixed, small amount
  (say $40).
- Cash goes out only with a receipt *at the moment of payout*.
- It gets counted monthly, on the record.
- It is not an "officer slush fund," a "tips jar," or a "surprise gift fund."

When in doubt: **close the box, move the float to Operating, and reimburse
normally.** You'll lose nothing and gain a clean trail.

## The monthly audit of yourself (30 minutes)

Once a month, with the report (Chapter 4), do these four checks:

1. **Every bank transaction that month has a ledger line.** Bank statement vs.
   ledger, line by line. Mismatches get investigated, not shrugged at.
2. **Every ledger line with money out has a receipt or an explainable story.**
3. **Every reimbursement paid had both a receipt and a separate approver.**
4. **Balances in the ledger match the bank to the penny.** If not, the
   difference gets a note: what it is, when it'll resolve, or an explicit
   adjustment.

You will find things. Finding things monthly in small amounts is the treasure
you're buying with 30 minutes. Finding things yearly in large amounts is the
alternative.
