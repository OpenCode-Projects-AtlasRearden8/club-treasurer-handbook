Chapter 7 — The Year-End Handoff Binder

Build the thing you wish the last treasurer had built.

The friendship-level mission of this whole handbook is: the next volunteer says yes. Nothing increases that probability more than a handoff binder that makes the job look easy from the outside — because to you, it will have been.

The binder (physical, or a folder that's equally findable)

Whether literal three-ring binder or a shared folder, the rule is the same: one place, labeled sections, current. The checklist template is a printable version of this chapter; use it as your table of contents.

Section by section:

  1. Start here (1 page). "Congratulations — here's the job in one page." The five keys, who to call, the report rhythm, the two accounts, one warm paragraph that says: you've got this.
  2. Accounts. Bank names, account purposes, signer list and when it was last verified, masked account numbers (last 4 only — this handbook never wants a full account number written in a binder), statement access, and how transfers actually get made.
  3. The ledger. This year's ledger (copy or export), plus how to keep it. Include the starter template as the blank next year's copy.
  4. Monthly reports. All twelve, in order, unaltered. This alone is your year on paper.
  5. Receipts & reimbursements. The structure that worked (folder layout, naming convention, the reimbursement form in use) plus this year's filed receipts — or a pointer to where they live if volume got real.
  6. Vendors. The vendor payment log with this year's payments, plus standing quotes/contracts and vendor contacts.
  7. Dues. The dues tracker at year end (paid / unpaid per member per cycle), the dues rhythm this year used, and the current dues amount.
  8. Bank year-end statement for each account, plus the December report.
  9. Contacts. Bank contact, president, webmaster/communications person, venue manager, anyone the money touches. Names + club-appropriate contact info only (no personal secrets; no passwords in the binder — see below).
  10. Decisions log. One page: dated one-liners of money decisions the club made this year ("voted 6-0 to buy the banner, $200, April"). This is how your successor answers "why do we spend on X?" without archaeology.

The great dry run: December (or whenever the year ends)

In the last month of the treasurer year, do a close-out:

  • Final monthly report posted (regularly!).
  • Reconciled the ledger to the bank through the last day.
  • Chase the last receipts; note anything unresolved honestly in the binder.
  • Copy the current templates back into the binder as "blank for next year."
  • Write the Start Here page for whoever is next — their name if

known. They're reading this at their most anxious moment; write to that.

  • Update the fraud-proofing checklist status:

signer list verified? Rotating signers? Anything rattle-y noted?

The handoff meeting (30 minutes, two people, no dread)

Sit down with the incoming treasurer and a beverage, walk the binder top to bottom, and count the cash together (Chapter 1's rule, closing the loop). Then the best part — do the first month together: they watch you pull statements and fill one report, then they do it while you nod. That's the whole transfer. You are done; they OWN it.

Afterward, the outgoing treasurer's duties:

  1. Remove yourself from bank access (a phone call or a form; do it the same week).
  2. Hand over every login that was yours — passwords reset by the new treasurer, not written in the binder. Which brings us to:

What never goes in the binder

  • Passwords or PINs. They get handed in person, one at a time, and then the new treasurer changes them. The binder says who has access, never what gets them in.
  • Full account numbers. Last 4 digits tell you what you need; the full number tells a thief what they need. If the club keeps records with full numbers (old checks, statements), those live in a locked drawer or a password-protected file — not in the circulating binder.
  • Members' personal trivia. Dues amounts are fine; medical notes, addresses someone wishes weren't in the club record — not fine.

The one-sentence year in review

Two accounts, one ledger, twelve friendly reports, receipts and approvals on everything, vendors logged and paid fairly, dues collected kindly, and a binder that says "you've got this" on page one.

If you did that, hand the binder over humbly, attend one meeting as a civilian with a snack, and enjoy the rarest of club treasures: a clean, complete, warm handoff.
