Chapter 2 — The Two-Account Setup

Money for this year, money for later. That's the whole model.

The single most clarifying thing a club can do is split its money into two places with two jobs. Not five accounts, not a home equity line of credit for trophy season. Two.

Account A: Operating

The operating account is the club's wallet. Money in (dues, event income, donations) goes here. Bills, reimbursements, and regular spending go out of here. This account should roughly break even over a year — in a healthy club, it hovers not-too-far from zero, or holds a small operating cushion (one month's normal expenses is a nice target).

Job description: this account pays for the club as it is.

Account B: Reserve

The reserve account is the club's savings. Here's where the club keeps money left over at year-end, a start-up fund for big purchases (a PA system, a deposit on a venue, new tables), and anything the club would hate to lose in a bad month. This account earns interest if your bank offers it, and — mostly — it is touched rarely and on purpose.

Job description: this account pays for the club as it will be.

Money moves from Operating to Reserve only twice a year (say, after the June event and after year-end close) and always by explicit decision: the report that month says, "We moved $400 from Operating to Reserve for the sound-system fund," and everyone can see it. That's it. That's the whole relationship.

Why two beats one (and three beats two, only sometimes)

  • One account = money fog. Beginners' clubs that keep everything in one pot can't answer "can we afford the thing?" or "did we already save for the thing?" You end up eyeballing the balance, which works until the month it doesn't.
  • Two accounts = mental categories made physical. "Is this spending from this year's money or our savings?" becomes an actual question with an actual answer, because the accounts literally won't let you spend savings without a transfer.
  • More accounts = more bookkeeping. A "Tournament Fund," a "Snack Fund," and a "Pens and Stickers Fund" each add a reconciliation chore every month. Start with two. If a genuinely big, long-term purpose emerges (a building fund, an equipment fund), add Account C on purpose — with its own line in the monthly report and a written note of what it's for. Otherwise, keep the two-account life.

Setting them up (or fixing what you have)

In a perfect world this happens at the bank, with your club's officers and a cup of coffee:

  1. Gather your club documents. Banks usually want: articles of association or similar founding papers (if your club has any), an employer ID number if the club has one, minutes or a charter naming the club, and the identified signers. (Asking the bank what they need is normal. Call first. This is a one-visit errand 90% of the time.)
  2. Name accounts clearly: Club Name — Operating and Club Name — Reserve. Clear names make the books read like sentences.
  3. Put at least two people on each account as authorized signers — you plus the president is the classic pairing. This is your fraud-proofing AND your "treasurer got flu at the worst time" backup in one move.
  4. Turn on statement delivery (paper or online) that the club can see, not just your inbox. A reserve account statement that only the treasurer has seen for two years is a trust hole with a bow on it.
  5. Order a debit card for Operating ONLY. The reserve account gets no card. Spending reserves requires an actual transfer you have to type on purpose. That friction is a feature.

The one-flow rule

Here's the flow that keeps the system honest, written as one sentence:

Money enters Operating for this year's work; anything the club decides to save moves to Reserve in a visible, minuted transfer; money leaves Reserve only by an on-the-record decision with a purpose.

If you internalize that one sentence, you're most of a treasurer already.

Good questions to ask the bank (they love it)

  • What do you need from us to open a small-club account, and to add or remove signers?
  • Is there a monthly fee, and at what balance is it free?
  • Can we get statements that two officers can access?
  • For the reserve account: is there a savings option with interest and no card, and how fast can we move money between accounts?

What two accounts will NOT fix

  • Spending that nobody recorded (that's the ledger's job — Chapter 3).
  • Decisions to spend savings that were never made on the record (that's the report's job — Chapter 4).
  • A signer list with one name on it who left the club in 2021 (that's a call to the bank, this week — see chapter 1).

Chapter checklist

  • Two accounts exist, with clear names and clear jobs
  • Two signers per account; club-accessible statements
  • Operating has the debit card; Reserve has none
  • Both accounts appear as lines in the ledger and on the monthly report
  • Transfers between them happen twice a year (or less), on the record
